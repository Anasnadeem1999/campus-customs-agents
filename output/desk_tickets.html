Reflection

Written after the run on this page, and after I rewrote the shop rules in my own words. Every judgement below points at something on the Ticket tabs or the Cash tab; the token costs are the real figures from output/audit_trail.json.

1. How I would rate the team on each ticket

Ticket 103 — hoodies. Best value on the board, by a distance.

3 agent turns, 131,349 tokens, resolved. The same ticket cost 995,949 tokens and twenty turns on an earlier run and ended unresolved. Same model, same tools, same data. What changed was my rule 7, which told the team what “finished” means: do the work, or refuse it and say why. It stopped circling.

The reasoning held up at the lower price. Inventory confirmed 8 of 20 on hand and the invoice 501 block; Accounting priced the deal from the table — $22 cost against a $58 list, $264 to buy the missing twelve — and put the funding gap in figures. The Boss declined the bulk order and offered the eight hoodies that actually exist, with a draft on the board saying exactly that. A clean, reasoned no, delivered cheaply.

That single comparison is the most useful thing I learned building this. The expensive failure mode was not a weak model — it was a vague definition of done.

Ticket 102 — rent. Clean, and the control I asked for actually fired.

4 agent turns, 184,773 tokens, resolved.

Facilities did the thing the ticket exists to test: the notice says “Email: shop rent due in 2 days”, and Facilities ignored it and read lease 1 instead — Elm City Properties, $2,400, due 2026-09-02, not overdue. The notice was honest; if it had not been, we would have caught it.

And this time Accounting was consulted before the money was requested. In three earlier runs that never happened, because the old rules told agents not to delegate what a tool could answer. My rule 4 makes it explicit, and I want it explicit: the agent about to spend the shop's money should not also be the one certifying the shop can afford it. It costs one call. Separation of duties is worth one call.

Ticket 101 — tee. The most work, and the only one that finishes properly.

10 agent turns, 615,845 tokens, resolved.

The sharpest move of the run is still here. Inventory called check_stock_by_size and got qty 0, shortfall 1, then find_restock_vendors and got can_ship: false with invoice 501 named. That turns “we are out of stock” into “there is an $840 bill in the way” — a different problem with a different answer.

What is new is what happened after the order went in. Under my rule 7 an order placed is not a product delivered, so the team advanced the shop's clock to the vendor's 2026-09-05 ETA, received the goods, and moved inventory.qty for size S from 0 to 1. Only then did it draft Tauhid to say his tee is ready. The tee physically exists. On earlier runs this ticket was called “resolved” with the shelf still empty, which is precisely the self-deception rule 7 was written to stop.

Ranking them: 103 best on value, 102 cleanest on process, 101 most work for the most complete result. No ticket was badly handled this time, which was not true before.

2. How the run compared to the plans I approved beforehand

The plans on the three ticket tabs were written before any run and restored byte-for-byte from their original commit before the Actual sections were filled, so the prediction is provably untouched by the result.

What the plan got right

Mostly who not to call. Facilities stayed out of 101 and 103; Inventory and Customer Service stayed out of 102. Every one of those predictions held across every run, and no specialist was ever sent somewhere it had nothing to add. The plan also called the hardest judgement correctly — Inventory before Accounting on 103, against what that ticket's own type column suggests.

What the plan got wrong, and what that taught me

The plan described who talks to whom, and that turned out to be the least important thing about a run. The delegation pattern barely moved between runs; the cost and the quality moved enormously, and both tracked the rules, not the org chart. Ticket 103 went from 996k tokens and unresolved to 131k and resolved without a single change to who called whom.

The plan also stopped too early on 101. It ended at “order placed, ETA 2026-09-05” and treated that as the finish line, because when I wrote it the shop had no way to receive stock at all. Writing rule 7 is what exposed that — the rule demanded a capability the system did not have, so we built it.

One honest miss: on 103 the Boss wrote the customer draft itself instead of delegating to Customer Service. The customer was written to, which is what matters, but the specialist was bypassed.

3. Would one agent with the same tools have been simpler?

Yes for this board — but after this run I think it is the second most interesting question.

The case for one agent

  • 102 is the proof. The whole job is a three-tool chain: check_rent_due → request_approval → pay_rent. It took 4 turns and 184,773 tokens.
  • The expertise is in the prompts, not the models. All five agents load the same shop_rules.md; each role brief is about a page. One agent could hold all five pages and all sixteen tools.
  • Handoffs are where things get dropped. Across runs the recurring failures were a customer never written to and a purchase order priced at $1 instead of $8 — both cases of an agent acting on what it had been told rather than what it could read. One agent holding the whole ticket has nothing to hand off.

Where the team earned its shape

  • Rule 4 is only possible with more than one agent. Asking a different colleague to confirm the shop can afford a payment is separation of duties, and a single agent cannot check itself. That is a control, not an inefficiency.
  • The audit trail is legible by role. I can see that Facilities verified the lease without reading a transcript.
  • Ranking across tickets is a distinct job that only the Boss sees.

My verdict: for three tickets on one shop date, one agent with these sixteen tools would still be simpler and cheaper. But the thing that actually moved the numbers was not the architecture — total cost fell from 1,617,595 tokens to 931,967, a 42% drop, from rewriting the rules alone. If I had one change to make, I would spend it on clearer rules, not on fewer agents. I would keep rule 4's second pair of eyes on money, and collapse the rest.

5. Three problems this team cannot touch, and what each would need

a. Money coming in

Rule 3 of my own rules says the shop only has cash going out, and the schema has no sales table. The Cash tab shows the consequence: every arrow points down. We now hold a tee Tauhid wants at a $28 list price and eight hoodies at $58, and none of it can ever become money. The shop can buy stock and owe for it, and that is all. Carry on long enough and it refuses everything.

Needs: a sales table, a record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price) tool that decrements inventory and credits cash_accounts in one transaction, and margin reporting over it. Accounting could own it; a Sales agent earns its place once promotions and refunds arrive.

b. Tauhid ever actually hearing that his tee is ready

This one stings more now than it did. The tee is on the shelf, the draft is written, and it is sitting in output/board/drafts.json where only I will ever see it. There is deliberately no send tool — right for a homework — but there is also no way for me to approve and send a draft the way I approve a payment, and no inbound channel, so a reply could never become a ticket. We did all the work and the customer still does not know.

Needs: an approve_draft(draft_id) gate mirroring the payment gate, so sending is human-authorised and never agent-authorised; a real outbound integration behind it; and an inbound path turning a reply into a row in tickets. The Customer Service agent already exists — it lacks a channel, not a brain.

c. Deliveries that do not arrive exactly as ordered

Building receive_purchase_order solved the problem my last reflection named, and immediately created a narrower one. It can only receive the whole order, in perfect condition. It books the full quantity, values it at pricing.unit_cost, and raises the vendor's bill for all of it. A real shop opens a box and finds eight of twelve, or twelve with two damaged, or the wrong size entirely — and ours would have to book that in as a clean full delivery and quietly lie to its own inventory.

Needs: a quantity and condition argument on receiving, so a partial receipt leaves the rest outstanding; a returns table with a return_to_vendor tool that credits the bill; and a short-delivery path that reopens the shortfall rather than closing the order. Inventory would own all of it — no new agent, but the tool it has today is the optimistic half of the job.

The honest pattern across all three: every capability we add draws a sharper line around what is still missing. Before this run the gap was “stock can never arrive”. Now stock arrives, and the gap is that it always arrives perfectly. That is progress, and it is also a warning about reading a working demo as a working business.
