Reflection

Written after the run on this page. Every judgement below points at something on the Ticket tabs or the Cash tab; the token costs are the real figures from output/audit_trail.json.

1. How I would rate the team on each ticket

Ticket 102 — rent. Best value on the board.

4 agent turns, 201,921 tokens — by far the cheapest of the three, and fully resolved.

It earns the mark on one decision. The ticket is only a claim: “Email: shop rent due in 2 days.” Facilities did not take it. It called check_rent_due and answered from lease 1 itself — Elm City Properties, $2,400, due 2026-09-02, two days out, not overdue. The notice happened to be honest; if it had not been, we would have caught it. A shop that pays what the invoice says without opening the contract overpays quietly for years.

Two colleagues, no wasted calls, and the lease rolled to 2026-10-02 so September cannot be billed twice. The Cash tab shows the clean $2,560 → $160 step.

Ticket 101 — tee. Resolved, but it took me to catch a real error.

12 agent turns, 685,733 tokens.

The sharpest move of the run happened here. Inventory called check_stock_by_size and got qty 0, shortfall 1, then find_restock_vendors and got can_ship: false with invoice 501 named. That turned “we are out of stock” into “there is an $840 bill in the way” — a different problem with a different answer. Paying it ($3,400 → $2,560) unblocked the vendor, the tee went on order, and Tauhid Zaman was drafted a reply carrying the real 2026-09-05 date.

But Accounting asked me to approve that purchase order at $1.00. pricing.unit_cost for CC-TEE-WHITE is $8.00 and the order is one unit; it had put the quantity in the amount field. I rejected it with that reason, the team re-raised at $8.00, and I approved that.

That is the most instructive thing that happened all run. The shop rules forbid stating a figure that did not come from a tool, and the team broke that rule in the one place it mattered. Nothing in the system caught it: request_approval checks invoice and rent amounts against the database, but a purchase-order amount is free text, so the tool had nothing to compare it to. The human gate was not a formality here — it was the only control that worked.

Ticket 103 — hoodies. Right answer, expensive route.

20 agent turns, 995,949 tokens — 53% of the run’s entire cost for a ticket where no money moved.

The reasoning was good. The Boss called Inventory first on a ticket typed price_override, which reframed it correctly: 8 of 20 on hand, shortfall 12, so this was never really a discount question. quote_discount gave the shape of the deal — $22 cost against a $58 list, and $264 to buy the missing twelve.

And this time the team asked me rather than deciding for me. It raised the $42 price, which I approved, and put the funding gap in figures so the restock could be refused explicitly. The ticket ends as a priced partial offer: eight hoodies at $42 that genuinely exist, twelve honestly declined, and a draft on the board saying exactly that. That is a finished answer, not a shrug.

Ranking them: 102 best on value and judgement; 101 second, strong detective work undercut by a pricing error it did not catch itself; 103 last — the right outcome, reached through five times the tokens of the ticket that actually moved money.

2. How the run compared to the plans I approved beforehand

The plans on the three ticket tabs were written before this run and restored byte-for-byte from their original commit before the Actual sections were filled, so the prediction is provably untouched by the result.

What the plan got right

Mostly who not to call. Facilities stayed out of 101 and 103; Inventory and Customer Service stayed out of 102. Every one of those held, and no specialist was sent anywhere it had nothing to add. The plan also called the hardest judgement correctly — Inventory before Accounting on 103, against what that ticket’s own type column suggests.

The most valuable thing the plan did was name Customer Service on 101. Earlier runs of this same board dropped that delegation and closed the ticket with the customer never written to. Having written down who should be called is what made that omission visible as a failure instead of passing for finished work.

What the plan got wrong

It assumed delegation where a tool call would do. The expected Facilities → Accounting hop on 102 would have spent a whole model call to fetch a number get_cash_position returns directly. Three runs have now skipped it. Writing the plan I was thinking about who owns which topic; running it, the team was thinking about what is cheapest to find out. The team was right.

It also under-called 103, treating the price override as optional on the grounds that pricing stock you cannot buy is premature. In practice approving $42 on the eight hoodies that do exist turned a refusal into an offer, which is plainly the better outcome.

3. Would one agent with the same tools have been simpler?

For this board, yes — and the numbers on this page say so.

The case for one agent

  • 102 is the proof. The whole job is a three-tool chain: check_rent_due → request_approval → pay_rent. It took 4 turns and 201,921 tokens, and two different agents asked get_cash_position the same question.
  • 103 is the stronger proof. 995,949 tokens to conclude “we cannot afford the restock” — something get_cash_position answers in one call. Most of that cost was the same figures re-read through four pairs of eyes.
  • The expertise is in the prompts, not the models. All five agents load the same shop_rules.md; each role brief is about a page. One agent could hold all five pages and all fourteen tools.
  • The $1 error is a handoff error. Accounting wrote an amount without reading pricing itself, because Inventory had already looked at the product and Accounting was working from what it had been told. One agent holding the whole ticket has the unit cost in front of it when it writes the number.

Where the team genuinely earned its shape

  • The audit trail is legible by role. I can see on the board that Facilities verified the lease, without reading a transcript. With one agent it is one long monologue.
  • Ranking across tickets is a distinct job. Rent before restock is the Boss’s call, and nobody else sees all three at once.
  • Separating who computes from who decides is a real organisational idea. Accounting prepares; the Boss and I dispose.

My honest verdict: for three tickets on one shop date, one agent with these fourteen tools and these shop rules would have been simpler, far cheaper, and less likely to misprice a purchase order. The team shape starts paying for itself at a board of thirty tickets, where specialists run in parallel and no single context holds everything — which is not the shop we have. I would keep the five prompts and collapse the five agents.

5. Three problems this team cannot touch, and what each would need

a. The tee actually arriving

Ticket 101 is resolved because the desk is finished — paid, ordered, customer told. Tauhid still does not have a tee, and with these tools he never will: there is no tool that receives stock and no tool that moves desk.date_today off 2026-08-31. inventory.qty for CC-TEE-WHITE/S will read 0 forever, and the ETA of 2026-09-05 can never arrive.

Needs: receive_purchase_order(po_id) — increments inventory.qty, writes the vendor’s bill into invoices, marks the order received; and advance_shop_date(to) so overdue tests and ETAs move with the calendar. No new agent — Inventory already owns stock.

b. Money coming in

Rule 8 of this build is that cash only goes out, and the schema has no sales table. The Cash tab shows the consequence: three tickets, every arrow pointing down. The eight hoodies we hold, at the $42 I approved this run, are $336 of revenue — three times the $104 gap that stopped the restock. The team cannot see that money, because no tool records a sale or credits cash_accounts. A shop that can only get poorer eventually refuses everything.

Needs: a sales table, a record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price) tool that decrements inventory and credits cash in one transaction, and margin reporting over it. Accounting could own it; a Sales agent earns its place once promotions and returns grow past one tool.

c. Tauhid ever actually hearing from us

draft_customer_message files a draft and there is deliberately no send tool — the right call for a homework. But there is also no way for me to approve and send a draft the way I approve a payment, and no inbound channel, so a reply can never become a ticket. The drafts sit in output/board/drafts.json where only I will ever look. Ticket 101 is marked resolved on the strength of a message that has not actually been sent.

Needs: an approve_draft(draft_id) gate mirroring the payment gate, so sending is human-authorised and never agent-authorised; a real outbound integration behind it; and an inbound path turning a reply into a row in tickets. The Customer Service agent already exists — what it lacks is a channel, not a brain.

The pattern in all three: these are not reasoning gaps. The team can work out that stock should arrive, that selling hoodies brings money in, and that Tauhid is waiting. It cannot act on any of it, because the shop is reached only through the MCP server and those verbs do not exist there. That restriction is the safety design working exactly as intended — and it is also precisely where the next problems live.
