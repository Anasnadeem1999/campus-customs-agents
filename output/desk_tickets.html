Reflection

My own answers, written after reviewing all three tickets. The figures are taken from output/audit_trail.json and the Cash tab of this page.

1. How I would evaluate the agents on each ticket

I think the team handled all three tickets well in the final run, although each ticket tested something different.

Ticket 101 — the most work

The agents did the most work here. Inventory found that the requested size-S tee was out of stock and that the vendor could not ship because of the unpaid $840 invoice. Accounting handled the payment approval, and the team ordered the tee, received it into inventory, and drafted a message for the customer. 11 agent turns, 619,229 tokens — the most expensive ticket, and the only one that had to move stock as well as money.

On an earlier run of this same ticket, Accounting requested approval for $1 instead of the correct $8 purchase order. I rejected it and the team corrected the amount. It did not recur in the final run, but it is the clearest thing I saw all project about why human approval matters — the tooling could not catch that error, because a purchase-order amount has no record in the database to be checked against. Only a person reading it could.

Ticket 102 — the cleanest process, and the cheapest

The team followed the process most cleanly here. Facilities checked the landlord's notice against the actual lease rather than taking the email at face value, and Accounting confirmed the amount and available cash before the payment was approved. The $2,400 rent was paid and the lease record was updated. It did this in 6 agent turns and 258,145 tokens, the least of any ticket.

Ticket 103 — the sharpest judgement

Inventory confirmed that only 8 of the 20 requested hoodies were available, while Accounting worked out that restocking the remaining 12 would cost $264. Since only $160 remained after the invoice and rent payments, the team correctly refused the restock and Customer Service drafted an offer for the 8 hoodies in stock. 7 agent turns, 371,465 tokens.

Ranking them

Ticket 102 comes first: it followed the process most closely and cost the least. Ticket 103 second, for the sharpest judgement call — refusing a spend the shop could not fund, and saying so in figures rather than half-promising. Ticket 101 third, for completing the most complicated task, though it took the most work to get there.

The main lesson for me is that the agents performed better when the rules clearly explained what they needed to do and when a ticket could actually be considered finished.

2. How the actual run compared to my expected plans

Ticket 101

Mostly followed the expected plan. The Boss involved Inventory first, then Accounting, followed by Customer Service. The actual result went further than I originally expected: the team did not stop at placing the order but advanced the shop date, received the tee into inventory, and drafted a message saying it was ready.

Ticket 102

Followed the plan closely. Facilities checked the lease first, and Accounting was involved before the rent payment was made. Both agents did what I expected and the $2,400 payment was processed correctly. There were no major differences between the plan and the actual result.

Ticket 103

Reached the expected outcome. The team checked inventory and pricing, recognised that the shop could not afford the additional $264 restock, and offered the 8 hoodies already in stock instead.

On the first attempt the Boss drafted the customer message itself rather than delegating it to Customer Service. The outcome was still appropriate, but the planned delegation was not followed, so I had the ticket re-run with the drafting handed to Customer Service where it belongs. In the final run all three tickets matched the plan.

Across all three

The plans were useful for deciding who should be involved and who could be left out. Every agent I expected to stay out of a ticket did stay out, on every run.

However, Ticket 101 showed me that the plan also needs to consider what happens after an order is placed. I had originally treated the order and its delivery date as enough, but the actual run made it clear that stock needed to be received before the ticket could genuinely be considered complete.

3. Would one agent with tools have been simpler?

For these three tickets, I think one agent with access to all the tools probably would have been simpler and cheaper. The tickets are closely related, and the same agent could check the records, work out the next step, and handle the ticket without passing information between different agents. Ticket 102 is a good example because the work was fairly straightforward: verify the lease, get approval, and pay the rent.

The handoffs also created opportunities for mistakes. Accounting requested the wrong amount for the tee purchase order, and on the first attempt at Ticket 103 the Boss drafted the customer message instead of involving Customer Service.

That said, I would keep some separation around financial decisions. Having another agent check affordability before money is committed provides an extra layer of control. It is easier to catch a mistake when the same agent is not responsible for every part of the decision.

The final run used 1,248,839 tokens across the three tickets, and the project records show that clearer rules reduced unnecessary loops compared with earlier runs — Ticket 103 alone had cost 995,949 tokens on an earlier run without finishing. So I would not assume that changing the number of agents alone would solve the main problem.

For this small shop, I would lean toward one main agent with the tools, while keeping human approval and an independent financial check where they matter.

4. Three problems this team could solve with the tools we built

1. Paying the new $8 vendor invoice

After the tee was received, Bulldog Print Co issued invoice 502 for $8. The team already has the tools to find the invoice, check the cash balance, request my approval, and pay it. Since the shop has $160 remaining, this payment is affordable. Paying it would also prevent another unpaid invoice from blocking future orders.

2. Restocking the Crest Mug

The Crest Mug is out of stock, but its vendor, Elm City Gifts, has no unpaid invoice blocking shipments. The team can check the stock, confirm the vendor's lead time, prepare a purchase order, get human approval, and receive the mugs when they arrive. At $3.50 per unit, this is a more affordable restocking option than the hoodie order.

3. Reordering the missing 12 hoodies once the shop has enough cash

The team has already established that the remaining 12 hoodies would cost $264. It cannot afford that order with only $160 in the account, but it has the tools to check the vendor, prepare the purchase order, and request approval once sufficient cash is available. The team should not place the order until the shop can afford it and the purchase is approved.

5. Three problems this team could not solve, and what would be needed

1. Recording sales and money coming into the shop

The current system tracks payments and other cash outflows, but it does not record sales revenue. For example, selling the tee or the 8 available hoodies would not increase the cash balance in the model.

What would be needed: a sales table and a tool to record sales, reduce inventory, and add the money received to the cash balance. Accounting could manage this, with a Sales agent becoming useful if the shop later needs to handle discounts, refunds, and sales reporting.

2. Actually sending messages to customers

The team can draft a message, but it cannot send it. This means Tauhid can have a tee ready for him while the message remains saved on the board.

What would be needed: a customer messaging integration and a separate approval step, so that a human approves a message before it is sent. An inbound channel would also help the team handle customer replies. The existing Customer Service agent could manage this once those tools are available.

3. Handling incomplete or damaged deliveries

The current receiving process assumes the full order arrives in good condition. In a real shop, a vendor might send only 8 of 12 hoodies or deliver items in the wrong size.

What would be needed: a receiving tool that records the actual quantity and condition, along with a way to record returns or damaged goods and adjust the vendor bill. Inventory could handle this without necessarily needing another agent, but the tools would have to support partial deliveries and returns first.
